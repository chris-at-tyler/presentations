What Still Needs a Harness?

The model is eating the harness. A field guide to what it can't digest — and what was never its food.

Logan Kilpatrick (Sequoia, Jun 2026) · Anthropic, "The New Rules of Context Engineering" (Jul 2026) · Sutton, "The Bitter Lesson" (2019)
Builds on part 2 (writing great skills) and parts 5–6 (context rules, MCP) · 2026-08

Frame the stakes in one line: half the room is writing skills and plugins, and the labs just told us they're deleting theirs. The talk answers three questions — what a harness is, what belongs in one, and what should have been a script all along. The punchline demo finishes part 2's makeover.
Act 1 · The Eating

"The model eats the scaffolding"

"The scaffolding is oftentimes a couple of steps ahead of what is baked directly into the model. And then what ends up happening is the model eats that scaffolding and it becomes part of the native model system." — Logan Kilpatrick, Google DeepMind (Sequoia Capital interview, Jun 2026)
  • "What we have historically thought of as the model is not just the weights anymore — it's an entire expanding, sprawling system built around them."
  • The forecast, with a date: everyone says the harness is where the alpha is — "that perhaps won't be true… in 12 months. It'll be upstreamed into the model."
Recap slide for anyone who missed the video-recap deck. Point out he gives a timeline — 12 months — which makes this checkable, not vibes. Hold the question "eats WHAT exactly?" — that's slide 4.

The deleted lines were rules like "default to writing no comments. Never write multi-paragraph docstrings…" — replaced by one line: "write code that reads like the surrounding code."

The third tile is the counterintuitive one — worked examples, the #1 old best practice, now measurably hurt. The eating isn't just "you may delete your guardrails" — it's "your guardrails have started to cost you." That asymmetry is what makes this urgent rather than optional housekeeping.

Act 1 · The Eating

Why the middle, specifically

Capability gaps get trained away.
Information gaps cannot be.

Trainable → temporary

"The model should have behaved differently"

Every behavior-correcting rule is a labeled failure mode — exactly what RL on agentic traces consumes. Once trained in, the rule is dead weight; a generation later, a cage.

Untrainable → permanent

"The model couldn't have known that"

No training run learns which library you banned last quarter, why the deploy freezes on Fridays, or what you mean by "clean." It has to be told. Forever.

The one-liner is the take-home sentence — if the room remembers one thing, this is it. Mechanism in one breath: your harness text and the vendor's training loop compete to fix the same failures, and the training loop compounds. That's why the eating is inevitable, not incidental.
Act 1 · The Eating

Sutton predicted it in 2019 — and drew the boundary

"We want AI agents that can discover like we can, not which contain what we have discovered." — Rich Sutton, The Bitter Lesson (2019)
  • 1Hands — actuation is physics; no gradient grows a filesystem
  • 2Information asymmetry — your private context isn't in the world's data at any compute budget
  • 3Authority — compute optimizes toward an objective; it doesn't pick one
  • 4Accountability — a guarantee is a property of a mechanism, not a skill level
  • 5Economics — a script's marginal run is free; inference never is
Hand-coded harness structure is exactly Sutton's "building in how we think we think" — helps short-term, satisfying, loses to training. But the essay's own last line concedes the limit: discovery reaches what's in the world; these five aren't. This is the theory floor under slide 5's one-liner. Note item 3 vs preferences: memory systems DO learn your taste over time — what they never absorb is that the call stays yours and can change on a whim. Content: slowly eaten. Authority: never.
Act 2 · Three Tests

Test 1 — Can you write the exact rule?

Then it's a script

Cheaper, faster — and more accurate.

A program's failure modes are enumerable. A model's aren't. An LLM at runtime doing a deterministic task is paying inference prices for a lookup table.

The model is the mechanic, not the engine

AI at authoring time and repair time — never run time.

Have the model write the script once, and fix the edge case that breaks it. Then the script runs forever, for free, the same way every time.

The mistake this catches: wrapping regex-shaped work in a skill. The eating thesis doesn't excuse it — script-shaped work was never the model's to eat.

This is the complaint that seeded the whole talk: people wrapping basic script work in skills and plugins because AI is the hammer in hand. Name it kindly — everyone in the room has done it, including me. The mechanic/engine line is the memorable half.

no: that's what a skill is for
  • Not "is it tokenizable" — almost everything is. The question is whether the agent can get to it without being handed it.
  • Every MCP connection shrinks skill territory: wire up the ticket system and "what the ticket requires" stops needing a skill.
  • The permanent residue: never-written knowledge (the why behind a convention, what the last outage taught) and taste.

Anthropic's version: "Avoid stating 'the obvious' things Claude should know by looking at your file system or your repo… spend most of the tokens on gotchas."

Reachability is an engineering variable that moves one direction — so context files need re-auditing the same way skills do, and the question is never "is this true?" but "can the agent now reach this without me?" Prefer connecting to copying: a pointer outlives a paste.
Act 2 · Three Tests

Test 3 — Does it need a guarantee?

Probabilistic asymptotes toward 100%.
It never reaches auditable.
  • CI gates, billing, security policy, compliance: the requirement isn't a high success rate — it's determinism, reproducibility, audit. Properties of a mechanism, not a skill level.
  • A task the model does at 99.5% still belongs to a program when someone has to prove what happened.
  • This is why the eating has a floor: a perfect model doesn't convert a gate into a judgment call — the gate's point is that nobody exercises judgment there.
The test people skip, because it doesn't feel like a capability question — it isn't one. Ask the room: would you accept "the model almost never approves a bad invoice"? The answer being obviously no IS the test. Governance and guarantees are the layers that thicken while orchestration thins.
Act 2 · Three Tests

The messy tail — and the eating that runs backwards

Deterministic core, AI escape hatch

For the 95%-rule-writable task with an endless tail.

The script handles the head of the distribution; the agent handles the tail; every tail case the agent solves is a candidate for promotion into the script. The boundary ratchets toward code.

Scripts are eating back

2023–24 ran the mistake in reverse.

Novelty pushed deterministic work into LLMs. The correction is symmetric: the model eats the orchestration middle, scripts reclaim the deterministic bottom — what stays custom is thin, declarative, and yours.

The escape-hatch pattern keeps the framework from reading as rigid — most real tasks straddle. The promotion loop is the practical habit: every time the fallback fires and succeeds, ask whether that case can move into the script. Equilibrium sentence sets up Act 3: what remains custom is thin, declarative, specific to you — which is exactly what a skill should now be.
Act 3 · The Skills Consequence

Two species of skills, opposite fates

Process skillsKnowledge skills
Form"follow these steps" · "you MUST" · all-caps enforcement facts, stated declaratively: the registry lives at X · we ban library Y since the incident · our OIDC apps use PKCE, no secret
What it really isorchestration in a skill costume unreachable context, hand-carried
Fateeaten — each model generation devalues it, then it goes negative survives every model generation
A skill full of MUSTs is a bet against the model.
A skill full of facts is a gift to it.
Anthropic says the survival criterion first-party: "It's best when skills encode particular opinions, knowledge, or best practices that are particular to you, your team, or product." The trajectory: imperative → declarative. A MUST that deserves to live is governance — a hard guardrail — and belongs to the permanent layers, not to process prose. Prune steps first; they age fastest.

  • What died was overwhelmingly process — restatements of what the model already does by default.
  • What survived is the knowledge fleet: infrastructure facts, org gotchas, domain reference.
  • The instrument — the sentence-level no-op test ("does this line change behavior vs the default?") — is model-relative: lines that passed last year fail after the next release. The eating, one sentence at a time.
Personal data, not a benchmark — say so. The 15/93 number lands hardest: five of every six descriptions were pure context tax. Challenge for the room: the no-op test needs re-running after every model upgrade, and the imperative sentences are where to start.

Wrap Up

Takeaways

  • The harness is eaten only in the middle. Sort by layer, not by slogan: hands, context, and governance stay; orchestration is food. Capability gaps get trained away; information gaps cannot be.
  • Three tests before you build. Exact rule → script (the model authors and repairs it — mechanic, not engine). Judgment → agent, fed only what it can't reach. Guarantee → program, at any model quality. Messy tail? Deterministic core, AI escape hatch, promote the wins.
  • Write skills as facts, not steps. MUSTs depreciate with every model release; unreachable knowledge never does. A skill full of MUSTs is a bet against the model; a skill full of facts is a gift to it.

This afternoon: run the existence test on one skill or automation you own — and the no-op test on whatever survives.
