How We Scaled Kimi K2.5

Zhilin Yang on three axes of scaling — token efficiency, long context, and agent swarms — and the optimizer, architecture, and RL techniques behind Kimi K2.5.

~40-min keynote, Kimi AI YouTube, uploaded 2026-03-21 · recap by Chris
Source: youtube.com/watch?v=CwePo4847ho · auto-captions, so names/terms are lightly hedged

Zhilin Yang is a Kimi/Moonshot AI researcher. The whole talk is one argument: there are three independent scaling axes beyond "more tokens" — token efficiency, context length, and agent count — and Kimi K2.5 is what you get when you push all three at once.
Open, Not Just Open · 0:07

Open models are catching up — that alone isn't the pitch

Why open matters

Deploy anywhere — local servers or cloud — and inspect every weight, instead of calling a black box.

The catch

Open models are closing the gap with proprietary ones, but "open models cannot be just open — they have to be great." 0:48

He credits the "closing the gap" chart to a Jensen Huang CES talk earlier in the year — a borrowed slide, not their own claim of a specific number.
The Three Axes · 1:25

Three scaling dimensions, in agent terms

Token efficiencya stronger prior for agent RL search → Long contextagents that run for days, weeks, months → Agent swarmsparallel sub-agents, not one agent alone

Standard scaling laws move you along one curve: more tokens, lower loss. This talk is about the other two knobs — moving that curve, and adding agent count as a third dimension entirely. 4:08

The "translate into agent terms" framing at 4:08 is the connective tissue for the rest of the talk — each of the three technical sections that follows maps directly back to one of these three boxes.
Token Efficiency Is Intelligence, Not Just Cost · 5:30

2x token efficiency ≈ "magically" having 2x the data

"Suppose you have 50 trillion high-quality tokens... apply a two-times-efficient optimizer... it's almost like magic that you get equivalently 100 trillion tokens." — 5:30

His framing: high-quality data is finite — the field is hitting a "data wall" — so squeezing more intelligence out of the same tokens is the lever, not a side benefit.

This slide is the setup for why the optimizer work (next slide) gets top billing rather than being a footnote about training speed.
The Optimizer · 6:12

Muon: a second-order optimizer, 2x token efficiency

What it does differently

Every gradient update is transformed so each entry is orthogonal to the others — unlike Adam's per-parameter updates.

Two techniques that made it scale

Weight decay, critical once models get large; and a tuned coefficient that keeps update magnitude (RMS) comparable to Adam's. 6:53

Claimed first published demonstration that this optimizer family is scalable for LLM training — plus a distributed implementation that partitions optimizer state across the data-parallel group to keep it memory-efficient at cluster scale. 7:34

Referred to in the talk as "Muon" (the transcript's auto-captions render it inconsistently, at one point as "Meow" — same technique).
Scaling To 1 Trillion Parameters · 8:14

The 1T-parameter cliff, and the fix

The problem

  • Max logits explode past 1,000
  • Normal range is roughly 50–100
  • Training loss diverges

The fix: QK-clip

  • Per attention head, per forward pass
  • Compute a dividing factor
  • Apply it to query & key projections
8:56

The result

  • Loss curve identical to unclipped
  • Clip relaxes naturally over steps
9:39

Shipped in Kimi K2's training — described as the first trillion-parameter-scale run of this optimizer family. 10:21

Nice detail for the room: the clip doesn't just cap the value and stay there — the network learns to keep itself under the clip threshold, so the constraint becomes unnecessary after enough training steps.
Long Context · 11:04

Long context is really an agent-runtime question

Transformers keep getting better the further into a long context they go; LSTMs saturate and stop improving. 11:46

Fine for machine translation a decade ago — but "not going to be accomplishable by LSTMs" if the task is understanding an entire codebase, or running an agent trajectory long enough to write a Linux kernel from scratch. — 12:27
This is the bridge slide into Kimi Linear — the architecture is the answer to "how do we get transformer-like long-context scaling without full attention's cost."
The Architecture: Kimi Linear · 13:07

A per-channel memory, not one global decay knob

The old limitation

Classic linear attention has a single scalar decay factor — you're stuck choosing between forgetting almost everything or retaining almost everything. 13:48

Kimi Delta Attention's fix

Decay becomes a diagonal matrix — one rate per channel. Some channels decay slowly (long-range memory); others decay fast (refresh with new information). 14:31

Mixes linear and full-attention layers at a 1:2:3 ratio; the chunk-wise GPU formulation is an exact rewrite, not an approximation — nothing is traded away for parallelism. 16:32

The infra challenge he flags: once decay is a matrix instead of a scalar, it can't simply be factored out of the recurrence — the matrix-inversion + cumulative-decay rewrite is what makes it parallelizable on GPUs at all.
Results · 17:13

First architecture claimed to beat full attention everywhere

  • Wins on both ends. Beats MLA and gated-delta baselines on short-context (MMLU-style) and long-context (RULER) tasks — while being cheaper to run. 17:13
  • The gap widens at scale. Efficiency advantage grows further once context reaches 1M+ tokens. 17:13
  • Short input, long input, long output — all three at once. Per the talk, the first architecture to outperform full attention across all three simultaneously. 17:55
Take the "first architecture to..." framing as the speaker's own claim, not an independently verified benchmark result — worth a caveat if repeating this externally.
Agent Swarms · 18:38

One orchestrator, many sub-agents — like a company, not a person

The org-chart analogy

An orchestrator (his word: "CEO") decomposes work across sub-agents — researchers, developers — then a second wave (fact-checkers, downloaders) assembles the final report. 19:21

Why it's faster

At matched task complexity, swarms cut execution time versus a single agent — scaling to 100–1,000 sub-agents keeps complex tasks inside an economically viable time budget. 20:00

Scales on four axes at once: input (parallel reading of many sources), output (e.g. a 100-page report), actions, and orchestration itself. 20:42

The "serial collapse" failure mode (next slide) is what happens when you skip the reward shaping and just let the model default back to doing everything itself.
Teaching Swarms To Coordinate · 21:24

Three reward terms, not one

  • 1Instantiation reward. Pushes the model to actually spawn sub-agents instead of defaulting to solo execution ("serial collapse"); its weight decays once the behavior sticks — 22:04
  • 2Finished reward. Without it, the model games reward #1 by spawning sub-agents that never finish — this rewards a high completion ratio instead — 22:45
  • 3Outcome reward. The standard "did the whole task succeed" signal, same as single-agent RL — 23:26
He's explicit that supporting this required new infrastructure too — parallel execution plus multiple concurrent reward functions isn't free to build.
Kimi K2.5 · 24:09

All three axes, one model — plus native vision

Muon-clip optimizer + Kimi Linear architecture + agent swarms → Kimi K2.5, released "over a month" before this talk. Demo capability: it can read a video and produce a website that replicates or style-transfers it. 25:31

First open model with native joint vision-text

Vision and text tokens are fused from 0% of training ("early fusion"), instead of pretraining text-only for ~20T tokens and bolting on ~2T tokens of vision afterward. Reported to outperform that "late fusion" approach. 27:31

Pretraining: 15T base tokens + another 15T, on Nvidia H100 nodes (2TB RAM + 8 GPUs via NVLink per node) — with zero loss spikes despite the new optimizer. 26:11

His example for why early fusion matters concretely: vision-to-code only works if vision and text share one representation space from the start — bolting vision on afterward doesn't get you that.
Two Modalities, Each Making The Other Better · 28:56

Vision improves text. Text improves vision.

Finding 1: vision → text

Training on vision-only RL tasks (counting, visual QA — no math or coding) still lifted performance on text-heavy tasks. 28:56

Finding 2: text → vision ("zero vision SFT")

With a strong enough text base, joint vision+text training reaches near-state-of-the-art vision performance using zero vision SFT data. 29:38

His framing of why this is notable: historically, bolting vision onto a text model hurt text performance — the claim here is that trained this way, the two modalities reinforce each other instead. 28:13

"Zero vision SFT" is the more surprising of the two claims — worth pausing on if presenting live, since it implies a lot of the vision-specific fine-tuning work other labs do may be unnecessary given the right pretraining recipe.
Next Up: Attention Residue · 31:48

Residual connections, rotated 90 degrees

Framed via two borrowed ideas: ResNet made arbitrarily deep networks trainable in the first place; and — credited to a point from Ilya Sutskever — "a residual connection is a variant of LSTM, just rotated 90 degrees," i.e. depth-wise recurrence instead of time-wise. 32:28

Residual, todayfixed addition of only the immediately previous layer's output → Attention Residueattention over ALL previous layers' hidden states, not just the last one

A block-attention variant groups layers (e.g. 4–16 per block) and only applies this across block boundaries, to keep the compute/memory overhead down. 35:11

This section is explicitly framed as a sneak peek / early research direction, not something already shipped in K2.5 — worth flagging as more speculative than the rest of the talk.
Results & A Mindset Shift · 35:51

+24% token efficiency, and old ideas keep giving

24% token-efficiency improvement — 50 trillion tokens behave like over 60 trillion — with the largest gains on coding, math, and reasoning benchmarks (GPQA, MATH, HumanEval). — 35:51

His closing reflection: Adam (2014), attention (8+ years old), and residual connections are all "ancient" by AI standards, yet still yielding big gains — a shift he credits to today's "scaling ladder" and benchmark rigor replacing the publish-an-idea-without-proof norm of a decade ago. 37:55

Nice line to quote directly if presenting live — it's the thesis for why revisiting "solved" building blocks (optimizers, attention, residual connections) is a legitimate research program right now, not just tinkering.
Wrap Up

If you only remember five things

  • Token efficiency raises the ceiling on intelligence — it isn't just a cost line. A 2x-efficient optimizer behaves like 2x the training data. 5:30
  • Muon needed QK-clip to reach 1 trillion parameters without max logits exploding past 1,000. 8:14
  • Kimi Linear swaps one global decay knob for a per-channel one — and is claimed to be the first architecture to beat full attention on short, long-input, and long-output tasks at once. 17:55
  • Agent swarms need their own reward shaping. Instantiation + finished + outcome rewards — or the model just reverts to running solo. 21:24
  • Kimi K2.5 fuses vision and text from token zero and reports each modality improving the other, including near-SOTA vision with zero vision SFT data. 29:38
