July 2026 · The Code Review Split

Should humans review all AI-generated code?

It looked like a binary. Everyone arguing had already stopped reading the code — and started reading something else instead.

Primary sources: Martin · Hashimoto · Booch · Sridharan · Lemmer-Webber · Glyph · Ronacher · Browne · Willison · Zhan

The claim of this talk is not that one side won. It's that the question as posed is unanswerable, and that the thing everyone actually changed was which artefact carries their trust. Four acts: how it happened, the reframe, the evidence, what to do.

Act 1 · The Split

The two famous opposites aren't opposed

Uncle Bob, read closely

"My agents write the unit tests. I don't review those. They also write the gherkin acceptance tests and the QA procedures. I review those."

Plus: periodic manual testing, audit-or-spot-check by criticality, hard caps on complexity because "messy code slows my agents down".

Hashimoto, read closely

Reads every line for Ghostty — where he's the accountable maintainer of a performance-critical terminal.

His own three principles are about splitting sessions and giving agents a way to verify their work. That's a tier judgement, not a universal rule.

Both are verification-first. Both scale scrutiny to stakes. They are two points on one spectrum that got framed as two camps.

This is the first crack in the binary. If you only read the headlines you'd never know Bob reads anything at all.

Four independent relocations of human attention — by people who believe they disagree with each other.

This is the talk's central slide. The debate was never read versus don't-read. It's: which artefact can carry a human's trust? That question is answerable per artefact, which is why it's a better one. Browne is the punchline — his headline says read less, and his reading volume never changed.
Act 2 · The Reframe

The gauntlet school says so itself

"The gauntlet proves the code meets the spec — it cannot prove the spec covers everything that matters. That's why the spec goes to you." — old-coder, the skill that productised "don't read the code"

So the most rigorous implementation of not reading code does not remove human reading. It relocates it — from the implementation to the specification — and documents that it has done so.

Zhan's objection lands exactly here: the artefact Bob picked — Gherkin/Cucumber executable specification — is a genre with a poor industry record, cited via the vendors who built businesses on it and then abandoned it. Relocating trust onto a weak artefact inherits the weakness.

If you take one thing away: any workflow claiming to have removed human judgement has hidden where it went. Find that artefact and you've found the real quality gate.
Act 2 · The Reframe

"Review" is one word for four jobs

Solved

1 · Bug-finding

Does it do the wrong thing? Humans were always bad at this — and it long predates AI.

2 · Absence of badness

Vulnerabilities, dead code, missed factorisations, quiet dependencies. You cannot test for a case you never imagined.

3 · Operability

Can you debug it at 3am? Only reading is known to build the model you need under pressure.

No mechanism

4 · Shared understanding

Acculturation, norms, a synchronised model of the system. Review's real product — and you cannot acculturate a model.

The decomposition is Glyph's. Once you split them, the headline question dissolves: each job has a different mechanism and a different degree of solvedness.

Bob and Booch aren't disagreeing about one question — Bob is optimising job 1, Booch is defending job 2. Everyone assumes the other three come along for free.

And note the shape of the risk: jobs 3 and 4 degrade without emitting a signal. Bugs page you. Vulnerabilities eventually surface. A codebase nobody understands announces itself once, late, as an unavoidable rewrite.
Act 3 · The Evidence

Humans can't find bugs by reading — measured in 2007

100lines of Python, one file — and reviewers were told where to look
3exploitable bugs planted: easy, medium, hard
20 hreviewer-hours spent by security researchers
1 / 3found reliably. The "hard" bug: never found by anyone

Yee & Wagner, Building Reliable Voting Machine Software (Berkeley PhD, 2007). Best-case conditions for review: tiny surface, known location, expert adversarial readers, ample time. ~5 lines per reviewer-hour.

The detail the debate always omits The "medium" bug that a paired team of security experts missed — Yee's own parenthetical: "(Note that this bug could be caught by a static type checker.)"
Both camps cite this study. Lemmer-Webber uses it to argue humans can't keep up with LLM volume — correct. But the same page argues that tooling catches what humans don't, which is the gauntlet's claim. The study supports both wings. It settles only that unaided human reading is a weak detector.
Act 3 · The Evidence

The axis that improved isn't the axis that matters

50 → 95%syntax / functional pass rate, 2023 → 2026
45–55%security pass rate — flat across every model generation and size
29%security pass rate for Java, the worst performer
"Models have become excellent at writing code that compiles. They've failed at writing code that's safe." — Veracode, Spring 2026 GenAI Code Security update

Veracode sells security scanning, so treat the levels as interested. The flat trend measured with a constant battery across generations is the load-bearing part — harder to manufacture than a level claim.

This is why it matters: the stated reason people read less is "as the agents get more reliable." That premise is true on the axis you notice — it compiles, it passes, it works — and flat on the axis you don't. The signal driving the behaviour change and the signal that should govern it have decoupled. That's the condition where drift feels earned and isn't.
Act 3 · The Evidence

And you can't tell how it's going

+24%speedup developers forecast before the trial
−19%actual: they were slower with AI tools
+20%speedup they still believed afterwards

METR randomised controlled trial: 16 experienced developers, their own repositories, 246 completed issues. A ~39-point introspection error, in the optimistic direction.

Play this straight: METR publishes an explicit list of claims it does not support, the tooling was early-2025, and their 2026 follow-up reportedly estimates ~+18% speedup. The sign flipped. The perception gap didn't. Anyone quoting "AI makes developers slower" as current fact is misusing this; anyone citing the reversal as vindication is dropping the part that survived.

The relevance to review: the decision to stop reviewing is made on a feeling that things are fine. This is the one input we know is unreliable, by about forty points.
Act 3 · The Evidence

Drift is structural, so willpower isn't a plan

Simon Willison · 2025-03-19 "My golden rule for production-quality AI-assisted programming is that I won't commit any code to my repository if I couldn't explain exactly what it does to somebody else."
Simon Willison · 2026-05-06 "as the coding agents get more reliable, I'm not reviewing every line of code that they write anymore, even for my production level stuff… And now I've got that feeling of guilt."

Fourteen months. No announced change of mind. Guilt intact — which means the standard survived and the behaviour didn't. Meanwhile AI-authored PRs already sit 4.6× longer in review: "review everything" isn't a policy being executed, it's one quietly failing while still on the books.

Lemmer-Webber's bobsled: "the vehicle is the LLM, you are the passenger… More than just a slippery slope, it is a pre-crafted journey." Not a fallacy — a repeatedly observed dispositional change.

The convergence nobody notices: Uncle Bob reaches "don't rely on humans reading" because humans are slow and weak at code. Lemmer-Webber reaches the same conclusion because humans get carried off by the gradient. Opposite premises, opposite sympathies, identical operational conclusion — put the enforcement in the machinery.
Act 4 · What To Do

Only one version of the question survives

TierDefinitionBefore code was cheap
AThrowaway. Answers a question, then dies.didn't exist — too expensive to write
BYou'd like it to work. Failure is annoying.rare
CIt had better work. Failure means real damage.where most effort went
DIf it's wrong, someone dies or a business ends.where the rest went
Browne's inversion "If your code is so important that every single line needs to be verified because it could get people killed — you should be writing an unbelievable amount of slop. Not to put in your product, but to verify your product."

Review used to be the best-value quality mechanism because tests were expensive too. Generation cost collapsed; human attention didn't. So the higher the tier of what ships, the more disposable code you should generate around it.

Everyone in the debate already tiers implicitly — Hashimoto for Ghostty, Bob "according to criticality", old-coder scaling gauntlet depth by risk. This just makes it explicit.
Act 4 · What To Do

The policy the debate forgot to write

TierWhat blocks the mergeWhat a human readsBlast radius
Anothing — don't review, don't merge, delete itnothing. Reading it is the wastezero by construction — no prod creds, no shared state
Bgauntlet green: tests, types, lint, changed-line coveragethe per-file diff summary, not the diffrecoverable by redeploy; feature-flag anything visible
Cgauntlet + a human-approved spec before code existsthe interface layer, exhaustively — APIs, types, schemas, migrations, auth boundaries. Not every line of implementationstaged rollout, reversible migrations, alerts before users

Middle column is the one the whole argument skipped. Tier C reads interfaces because they constrain everything downstream — a small read with large coverage.

Tier C is where most professional work lives, and it explicitly does not require reading every line. That's the concession to the gauntlet school, and the evidence supports it. Note tier D reads everything while knowing full reading is a poor bug detector — because there you're buying something else.
Act 4 · What To Do

Three rules, and two things this still doesn't fix

  • 1Assign the tier before the work starts. Under delivery pressure everything reclassifies downward. Decide while you're calm.
  • 2Gates block; they don't warn. A warning is a request for willpower — and willpower is the input the evidence says gets withdrawn.
  • 3Review is budgeted or it doesn't exist. Name the person, the hours, and what happens to the release if they don't finish. Organisations reward output volume and cannot reward review — so a PR too big to review gets approved because it's too big.

Still open · Operability

Bought at C and D, abandoned at B — fine until a tier-B service becomes load-bearing without being reclassified.

Still open · Shared understanding

No per-change gate can produce it. Needs its own standing mechanisms: design docs humans argue about, architecture review of intent, rotating ownership, duplication metrics.

Rule 3 is the one that decides this in practice, and it isn't an engineering question at all. Also worth saying out loud: above some tier a regulator has already answered — EU AI Act high-risk duties, and DO-178C's requirement that every line trace to a requirement and back, which a no-human-read workflow structurally cannot produce.
Wrap Up

What actually came out of the argument

  • Nobody stopped reading — the trust moved. Gherkin, a SPEC, the interface layer, a generated walkthrough. Ask which artefact carries the trust, not whether to read. Any workflow claiming to have removed human judgement has hidden where it went.
  • Review was four jobs. Bug-finding is being automated. Security is unsolved by both camps. Operability and shared understanding have no mechanism at all. And those two degrade without emitting a signal — that's the real risk.
  • Don't build policy on discipline. The most careful practitioner in the field reversed himself in fourteen months and felt guilty about it. Both camps converge here from opposite premises: put enforcement in the machinery.
  • The more critical the code, the more slop you should generate around it. Cheap code inverts the safety argument. Spend attention on interfaces; spend generation on harnesses.

Unanswered by anyone: is an agent-generated walkthrough equivalent to acquired understanding under incident pressure? And can the coherence tax be instrumented as a duplication trend? The debate's largest claim is entirely unmeasured.

Close on the open questions rather than a verdict — the honest position is that two of the four jobs have no answer yet, and the field hasn't even tried to measure the biggest one.
